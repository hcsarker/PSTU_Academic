Probability Mass Function of Poisson Distribution: The probability mass function (PMF) of the Poisson distribution, denoted by P(X = k), represents the probability of observing k events in a fixed interval of time or space. The PMF of the Poisson distribution is given by:

P(X = k) = (e^(-λ) * λ^k) / k!

Where:

  • X is a random variable representing the number of events.
  • λ is the average rate or intensity of events occurring in the given interval.

(i) Exponential Growth and Exponential Decay: In the case of exponential growth, the number of infections increases rapidly over time. The exponential growth curve starts slowly but then accelerates, resulting in a steep upward curve. This pattern can be observed during the early stages of an infectious disease outbreak when the infection rate is high and there are no significant interventions or limitations on transmission.

In contrast, exponential decay occurs when the number of infections decreases rapidly over time. The exponential decay curve starts steeply but gradually levels off. This pattern can be observed when effective control measures, such as vaccination campaigns or strict public health interventions, lead to a decrease in the transmission rate and a decline in the number of new infections.

(ii) Herd Immunity and Tapering off Infections: Herd immunity refers to a situation where a sufficiently large portion of the population becomes immune to a disease, either through vaccination or prior infection. When herd immunity is reached, the transmission rate decreases significantly, causing the infection curve to taper off. This means that the number of new infections per unit time decreases, eventually leading to a decline in the overall number of active cases.

(iii) Rise in Cases if Restrictions Are Lifted Too Soon: If restrictions, such as lockdowns or social distancing measures, are lifted prematurely or without proper precautions, the transmission rate can increase again. 6(b) Application of the SIR Model: The SIR model is a mathematical model used to study the spread of infectious diseases in a population. It divides the population into three compartments: susceptible (S), infected (I), and recovered or immune (R). The SIR model helps understand how infectious diseases progress and provides insights into epidemic dynamics.

Schematic of the SIR Model:

  • Susceptible (S): Individuals who are susceptible to the disease and can become infected.
  • Infected (I): Individuals who are currently infected and can transmit the disease to susceptible individuals.
  • Recovered or Immune (R): Individuals who have recovered from the infection and are immune to reinfection.

The SIR model represents the flow of individuals between these compartments using a set of differential equations. It considers parameters such as the transmission rate (β) and the recovery rate (γ). The model assumes that the total population size remains constant and that individuals can only transition from the susceptible to infected state and then to the recovered state.

Maximum Number of Infected (Imax) and Solution Curves: In the SIR model, the maximum number of infected individuals, denoted as Imax, represents the peak of the epidemic curve. Imax depends on various factors, including the initial conditions, transmission rate, and recovery rate. It indicates the highest number of active infections during the outbreak.

Solution curves in the SIR model describe the temporal evolution of the compartments (S, I, and R) over time. They illustrate how the number of susceptible individuals decreases as infections occur, the number of infected individuals reaches a maximum, and the number of recovered or immune individuals gradually increases. The shape of the solution curves depends on the specific parameter values and initial conditions chosen for the model.

Note: The SIR model provides a simplified representation of disease spread and assumes homogeneous mixing within the population. Real-world scenarios may involve additional complexities and variations, requiring more sophisticated models.
